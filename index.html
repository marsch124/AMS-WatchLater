How this works
Four ways to get a video in. They all end up in the same list, so use whichever is nearest to hand.

1 — Add to WatchLater, from Raycast or Alfred. With a video open in Safari, type its name and press Return. Nothing moves, nothing opens, the page stays exactly where it was; a notification tells you what was saved. This is the one to build the habit around.
2 — A hotkey, if you want one. Raycast can give any app a key combination in its own settings — pick Add to WatchLater there and press the keys you want. Nothing else to install.
3 — Paste. Drop one link or fifty into the box at the top, one per line, and press Add. This is how you empty a window full of tabs in one go.
4 — Drag and drop. Pull a link from anywhere onto this window.

A whole playlist in one go. Paste a playlist link — the kind that is only …/playlist?list=…, with no single video in it — and every video in it is read off the playlist page and added, in the playlist's own order, up to sixty. A normal video link that happens to carry a playlist on the end still saves just the video you were watching, which is how it has always worked.

The row of buttons is the whole idea. You almost never want to know what you saved on Tuesday. You want to know what fits in the twenty minutes you actually have. Click a time and the list shows only videos that fit inside it. Everything shows the lot. That one change is what turns a saved list into something you open.

The row also tells you how much is there. Beside the times you get, for whatever is showing, something like 9 videos · 2 h 40 min. It is the difference between “a few things saved” and “that is a whole evening” — and it is usually the sentence that makes you either pick one now or clear a couple out. A + at the end means one of them has no length yet, so the real total is a little higher.

A line about why you saved it. Under the title of every card there is room for one line in your own words — for the trip, the bit about sleep, watch with Lisa. Click it and type; Return saves, Escape forgets it. Three weeks later the title alone rarely tells you what you were after, and that is exactly when a video gets skipped over instead of watched. It is one line on the card on purpose — nothing here deserves a screen of its own.

Pinning. The pin in the corner of a picture holds that video at the top of the list, above everything else, until you press it again. For the one or two you have actually decided to watch next, rather than the pile you might.

Tags — your own words. Click the note line under a title and you get two boxes: the line about why you saved it, and under it tags, separated by commas — AI, Woodworking, Sport. Tags you have already used appear as dashed suggestions; clicking one adds it, which keeps the same idea from ending up spelled three ways. Every tag you use turns up as a filter under Sort, with a count, and the tags on a card can be clicked too. Channels tell you who made a video; tags tell you what it is for, and only you know that.

Doing several at once. Hover a card and a tick box appears in the top-left corner of its picture. Pick a few and a green bar appears above the list: tick them all off, or remove them all. Both can be undone for five seconds, same as one at a time. This is for the evening you look at the list and realise nine of them are never going to be watched — one pass, done, instead of nine little decisions.

“Did you finish it?” When you open a video from here, the app remembers you started it. Next time you look at the list that card asks, once, whether you finished it. Yes ticks it off; Not yet leaves it alone and stops asking until you open it again — and the card then says Started three days ago rather than Saved last month, so a video you are halfway through never looks the same as one you have not touched. That gap is where half-watched things used to disappear.

Checking a card is still true. Titles get rewritten, videos get made private, thumbnails get swapped. Pick one or several with the tick box and press Check in the green bar: the app asks YouTube again and brings the title, channel, length and picture up to date. Anything that has been taken down is marked Not on YouTube any more in amber rather than deleted — throwing it away is your decision, not the app's.

Finding one particular video. Type into the Search box, top right. It looks at titles and channel names as you type, and it ignores accents — hjarnans finds hjärnans, kaffet finds En AI till Kaffet. Because you are now looking for one thing rather than asking what fits in twenty minutes, the row opens up to Everything on its own, so the video cannot be hiding behind a slot that is too short. Escape, or the little cross, clears it.

Only this channel. Click the channel name under any title — Raycast, Big Think — and the list narrows to that channel alone, with its name shown as a green tag under Sort. Click the tag to go back to everything. It is the quickest way to line up three videos from the same person and take them in one sitting.

Sort. Newest is the normal order, with anything the three-month nudge has flagged floated to the top. Shortest and Longest reorder by length — and Longest with a time slot picked is the useful one: it shows what fills the twenty minutes best, rather than the four-minute clip you would have got through anyway. Pinned videos stay on top whichever you choose.

Cards or Compact. Cards is the picture-first layout. Compact shrinks each picture to a strip down the side and stacks the rows up, so roughly three times as many fit on the screen — the one to use when the list has grown and you are scanning rather than browsing. This Mac remembers which you chose, along with the sort. Compact leaves out the empty why did you save this prompt to save a line; notes you have already written still show, and you can add one in Cards.

Lengths arrive on their own. When a link comes in, the length is read straight off the YouTube page, here on this Mac. There is no account, no key and nothing to set up. If a video has been made private or taken down the length cannot be read, and rather than saving a blank card the app tells you it could not read the link. For anything that is not YouTube, the card asks you to tap a rough length so it still lands in the right time bucket.

Nothing rots quietly. Anything sitting untouched for three months turns amber and asks whether you still want it. Keep resets its clock for another three months; the bin removes it. Without this, a read-later list only ever grows, and a list that only grows is one you stop opening. That is what happened to every other one you have tried.

Ticking one off. The tick marks a video watched and takes it out of the list. Watched in the button row brings them back into view if you want to find something a second time.

Nothing asks “are you sure”. Tick something by mistake, or bin the wrong card, and a small message appears at the bottom with Undo for five seconds. A question you have to answer every single time stops being read after the first week and catches nothing; five seconds of undo actually does. While that offer stands the video is only marked, not gone — so even closing this window mid-undo cannot lose it.

It shows up on the Main Hub. The hub's small tiles now include one for this list: how many videos are waiting, and how many of them fit into five minutes. The hub only ever asks for those numbers — never a title, a link or anything about what you are watching — and only from the hub's own addresses. On the Mac, open the hub from the Dock and the tile fills itself in; the published copy on the web cannot reach this Mac, so there it stays quiet.

The engine. A small program runs quietly in the background to do the lookups and hold the list. You never have to start it: it comes up when the Mac starts, and both apps start it themselves if it is ever not running. The first video of the day may take a second or two longer while it wakes up.
Your data
Everything lives in one file on this Mac — watchlater.json, in the AMS WatchLater folder. Thumbnails sit next to it in thumbs, so pictures still show with the internet off.

A dated copy is written into the backups folder every time the list changes, and the last fourteen days are kept. That copy refuses to overwrite a fuller one from the same day, so a bad moment cannot quietly replace a good list with an empty one.

Back up now takes an extra copy, stamped with the time, and never touches the daily one — pressing it can only ever add a safety net. Every copy is listed below with its date and how many videos are in it, and Put this back restores one. A restore reads and checks the whole file before anything is written, so a damaged copy leaves your list alone; and the videos that were here are saved to their own copy first, so a restore can itself be undone. It tells you exactly what it did afterwards.

Nothing is uploaded anywhere and there is no account. The only thing that ever leaves this Mac is the lookup that fetches a video's title and length.
